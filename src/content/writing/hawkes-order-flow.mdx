Look at trades on any exchange at the scale of seconds and they do not arrive like raindrops. They come in
bursts: a large order sweeps the book, algorithms react to it, other algorithms react to those. The standard model
for "events that make more events" is the Hawkes process [1], best known outside finance through its
epidemic-type versions for earthquake aftershocks [6].

## From Poisson to self-excitation

A Poisson process has a constant rate: the chance of an event in the next instant ignores the past. A Hawkes
process lets every event raise the rate for a while:

$$
\lambda(t) = \mu + \sum_{t_i < t} \alpha\, e^{-\beta (t - t_i)} .
$$

$\mu$ is the background rate (events caused by something outside the process, such as news), $\alpha$ the jump in
intensity after each event and $1/\beta$ how long the excitement lasts.

The process has a family-tree reading [2]. Background events are immigrants; each event, immigrant or not, has on
average

$$
n = \int_0^\infty \alpha e^{-\beta s}\, ds = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}
$$

children. $n$ is the **branching ratio**. If $n < 1$ every family dies out and the process is stationary, with

$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{rate}] = \frac{\mu}{1 - n}, \qquad \text{mean family size} = \frac{1}{1-n} .
$$

The branching ratio is also the share of events that were triggered by earlier events rather than by the
background. At $n = 0.9$, nine trades in ten are reactions, and a single outside event sets off ten on average.

## Simulating and fitting

**Simulation by thinning** [3, 4]. Between events the intensity only decays, so its current value is an upper
bound until the next event. Draw a candidate time from a Poisson process at that bound, accept it with probability
$\lambda(t)/\bar\lambda$, update, repeat.

**Maximum likelihood.** For events $t_1 < \dots < t_N$ on $[0, T]$,

$$
\ln L = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \ln \lambda(t_i) - \int_0^T \lambda(t)\, dt ,
$$

and for the exponential kernel the sum has a recursion [5], $A_i = e^{-\beta(t_i - t_{i-1})}(1 + A_{i-1})$ with
$\lambda(t_i) = \mu + \alpha A_i$, so the likelihood costs one pass over the data.

On a simulated process with $\mu = 1$, $\alpha = 0.8$, $\beta = 1.2$ ($n = 2/3$) over 2,000 seconds, the
simulation produced 6,076 events (theory: 6,000) and the fit returned $\hat\mu = 1.02$, $\hat\alpha = 0.82$,
$\hat\beta = 1.23$, $\hat n = 0.66$.

```python title="hawkes.py"
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

def simulate(mu, alpha, beta, T, seed=1):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    t, excess, events = 0.0, 0.0, []
    while True:
        bound = mu + excess                      # intensity only decays until the next event
        wait = rng.exponential(1 / bound)
        t += wait
        excess *= np.exp(-beta * wait)
        if t > T:
            return np.array(events)
        if rng.uniform() * bound <= mu + excess:  # accept with probability lambda(t) / bound
            events.append(t)
            excess += alpha

def neg_log_likelihood(log_params, t, T):
    mu, alpha, beta = np.exp(log_params)
    if alpha >= beta:
        return np.inf                             # keep the branching ratio below 1
    A, log_sum = 0.0, 0.0
    for i in range(len(t)):
        if i:
            A = np.exp(-beta * (t[i] - t[i - 1])) * (1 + A)
        log_sum += np.log(mu + alpha * A)
    compensator = mu * T + alpha / beta * np.sum(1 - np.exp(-beta * (T - t)))
    return compensator - log_sum

t = simulate(1.0, 0.8, 1.2, T=2000)
fit = minimize(neg_log_likelihood, np.log([0.5, 0.5, 1.0]), args=(t, 2000), method="Nelder-Mead")
mu, alpha, beta = np.exp(fit.x)
print(f"{len(t)} events; mu {mu:.2f}, alpha {alpha:.2f}, beta {beta:.2f}, branching {alpha / beta:.2f}")
```

## A real burst

The "Real trades" tab shows six minutes of trades in one perpetual future on Binance, centred on a funding
settlement where the payment was unusually large (−1.28% for that four-hour interval). For most of the three
minutes before the settlement the flow ticks along at about three events per second; it picks up in the last
half-minute, and in the twenty seconds after the settlement there are about ten times as many.

Fitting the model to the whole window gives a branching ratio of $0.67$ and an excitement that decays in about
$0.16$ seconds. The fit is much better than a Poisson process (the log-likelihood improves by more than a
thousand), and the time-rescaling check passes [6]: transformed by the fitted intensity, the gaps between events have
mean 1.00 and variance 0.99, as unit exponentials should.

Fit only the calm three minutes before the settlement and the branching ratio drops to $0.22$, with a decay time
of about 13 milliseconds.

<Callout label="Read the branching ratio with care">
Part of the 0.67 is not reflexivity at all. The settlement is an outside clock that lifts the background rate,
and a model with a constant $\mu$ can only explain the burst by making events excite each other more. A
branching ratio is a measure of endogeneity only when the background has been modeled honestly; regime shifts
and outliers are known to push its estimate upward, even toward 1 [8].
</Callout>

## Where it is used

Hawkes processes appear wherever events beget events: aftershocks after an earthquake, retweets, and in markets,
models of order arrival and of price jumps that cluster [9], and of how close a market is to instability. Filimonov
and Sornette used the branching ratio as an index of how much of a market's activity is self-generated [7]; a ratio
drifting toward 1 means a market that mostly reacts to itself.
