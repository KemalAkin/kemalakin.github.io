## The model

Split a population into the susceptible $S$, the infectious $I$ and the recovered $R$. Infections happen when
susceptible and infectious people meet, at a rate $\beta$ per day, and the infectious recover at a rate
$\gamma$. Working in shares of the population (so $S + I + R = 1$) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dS}{dt} &= -\beta S I \\[1.2ex]
\frac{dI}{dt} &= \beta S I - \gamma I \\[1.2ex]
\frac{dR}{dt} &= \gamma I
\end{aligned}
$$

<Callout label="Counts or shares">
With head counts instead of shares the infection term is $\beta S I / N$, where $N = S + I + R$ is the
population size. Dividing every equation by $N$ gives the form above.
</Callout>

The second equation already holds the main result. $I$ grows only while $\beta S > \gamma$, that is while
$R_0 S > 1$ with the basic reproduction number $R_0 = \beta / \gamma$. Every infection uses up susceptibles,
so $S$ falls until the condition fails and the curve turns over.

## Integrating it

There is no closed-form solution for $I(t)$, so we integrate numerically. SciPy's `solve_ivp` does it in a few
lines. The figure above uses a hand-written fourth-order Runge–Kutta step instead, which is the same idea in
about fifteen lines of JavaScript.

```python title="sir.py"
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

def sir(t, y, beta, gamma):
    s, i, r = y                     # shares of the population
    return [-beta * s * i, beta * s * i - gamma * i, gamma * i]

beta, gamma = 0.32, 0.10            # per day
y0 = [0.999, 0.001, 0.0]
t = np.linspace(0, 160, 641)

sol = solve_ivp(sir, (0, 160), y0, args=(beta, gamma), t_eval=t)
s, i, r = sol.y
print(f"R0 = {beta / gamma:.2f}, peak on day {t[i.argmax()]:.0f}")
```

## How big does it get?

Divide the first equation by the third and integrate. With almost everyone susceptible at the start, the share
that is still susceptible at the end, $s_\infty$, solves

$$
s_\infty = \exp\!\big(-R_0\,(1 - s_\infty)\big)
$$

For $R_0 = 3.2$ that leaves about 5% untouched, so roughly 95% are infected at some point. Drag $\gamma$ up
until $R_0$ drops below 1 and the outbreak never takes off.
