A random walk has no memory of where it has been. Many things in markets do: the gap between two closely related
prices, an interest rate, the premium of a perpetual future over spot. They wander, but they get pulled back. The
standard model for that pull came from physics, and it has a trap built into how it is usually estimated.

## A particle with friction

In 1908 Langevin wrote down Newton's law for a small particle in a fluid: a viscous friction force proportional
to its velocity plus an irregular force from the molecules around it [1]. Uhlenbeck and Ornstein worked out the
statistics of its solution in 1930 [2]. Written for a
quantity $X$ that is pulled toward a level $m$, the equation is

$$
dX = -\theta\,(X - m)\,dt + \sigma\,dW .
$$

The first term is the friction: the further $X$ is from $m$, the harder it is pushed back, at rate $\theta$.
The second is the same Brownian noise as in geometric Brownian motion. The solution is a Gaussian process with

$$
\mathbb{E}[X_t] = m + (X_0 - m)\,e^{-\theta t}, \qquad
\operatorname{Var}[X_\infty] = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}, \qquad
\operatorname{Corr}(X_t, X_{t+\tau}) = e^{-\theta \tau}.
$$

Deviations decay exponentially, so the natural summary is the **half-life** $\ln 2 / \theta$: how long it takes,
on average, for a gap to close halfway. Noise and friction balance at a stationary spread $\sigma/\sqrt{2\theta}$,
the same balance that sets a Brownian particle's thermal velocity (the fluctuation–dissipation relation).

## Where it shows up in markets

- **Spreads between related assets.** Two assets driven by the same thing can drift apart, but not for long.
  The spread is modeled as OU; a trade opens when it is a couple of stationary standard deviations wide.
- **Interest rates.** Vasicek's 1977 short-rate model is exactly this equation [3].
- **Futures basis and funding.** A perpetual future is tied to spot by a funding payment that pushes the
  premium back toward zero. The premium behaves like a spring with noise.

## Estimating it

Sampled every $\Delta$, the OU process is exactly an autoregression:

$$
X_{t+\Delta} = m + b\,(X_t - m) + \varepsilon_t, \qquad b = e^{-\theta\Delta},
\qquad \operatorname{Var}[\varepsilon] = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}\left(1 - e^{-2\theta\Delta}\right).
$$

So the usual recipe is: regress $X_{t+\Delta}$ on $X_t$, read off the slope $\hat b$, and set
$\hat\theta = -\ln \hat b / \Delta$. It is short, it is in every tutorial, and on a year of daily data it lies.

## The trap

Feed the recipe a **pure random walk** ($\theta = 0$, no pull at all), one year of daily points, and repeat
20,000 times:

- 96% of the samples give a slope $\hat b < 1$, i.e. "mean reversion";
- the median implied half-life is **40 trading days**;
- 85% of samples report a half-life under six months, short enough to look tradeable.

When there *is* reversion the estimate is still too fast. With a true $\theta = 5$ per year (half-life 35
trading days), the median estimate is a half-life of 20 days.

This is a known small-sample bias of the autoregression slope, studied since Hurwicz [4] and worked out to first
order by Kendall and by Marriott and Pope [5, 6], and it is the reason unit-root tests such as Dickey–Fuller need
their own critical values [7]. With the mean estimated from the sample, the first-order bias is
$\mathbb{E}[\hat b - b] \approx -(1 + 3b)/n$, which puts the spurious speed of a random walk at about
$4/T$ per year for $T$ years of data. That matches the simulation, and it contains the uncomfortable part:

<Callout label="Sampling faster does not help">
The bias in $\hat\theta$ depends on the time span $T$, not on the number of points. One year of daily data
and one year of data eight times a day give the same spurious speed (median about 4.3 per year in both runs).
Four years of daily data bring it down to about 1.1. Mean reversion is learned from how many half-lives you
have watched, not from how finely you watched them.
</Callout>

## The trap on real prices

The "ETH/BTC" tab runs the same recipe on the ratio of two crypto prices, Binance perpetual daily closes from
September 2021 to October 2026. Over those five years the ratio fell from 0.078 to 0.029: whatever it is, it
did not revert. An augmented Dickey–Fuller test on the full series agrees ($p = 0.69$, no evidence of mean
reversion), and the full-sample fit gives a half-life of 524 days.

Now fit one-year windows, as a trader looking at "the last year" would. Across 101 windows, 15 days apart, the
median fitted half-life is **60 days**, and 89% of windows report a half-life under six months. The augmented
Dickey–Fuller test rejects a random walk in only 3% of the same windows. The regression finds a spring almost
every time; the test almost never does.

## What to do instead

1. **Test before you fit.** Run an augmented Dickey–Fuller test (or a cointegration test for a pair) with its
   proper critical values, not a regression $t$-statistic read against a normal table.
2. **Count half-lives, not rows.** Ask how many times the estimated half-life fits in the sample. Fewer than
   about ten and the estimate is mostly bias.
3. **Correct or simulate.** Add the first-order correction back to $\hat b$, or calibrate by simulating the
   fitted model and re-estimating it many times; the spread of those estimates is your real error bar.
4. **Hold out time.** Fit on one period and check that the spread actually reverts in the next one.

## Code

```python title="ou_bias.py"
import numpy as np

def simulate_ou(theta, sigma, n, dt, reps, rng):
    b = np.exp(-theta * dt) if theta > 0 else 1.0
    sd = sigma * np.sqrt((1 - b**2) / (2 * theta)) if theta > 0 else sigma * np.sqrt(dt)
    x = np.zeros((reps, n))
    shocks = rng.standard_normal((reps, n)) * sd
    for t in range(1, n):
        x[:, t] = b * x[:, t - 1] + shocks[:, t]
    return x

def fitted_theta(x, dt):
    x0 = x[:, :-1] - x[:, :-1].mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    x1 = x[:, 1:] - x[:, 1:].mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    b = (x0 * x1).sum(axis=1) / (x0**2).sum(axis=1)
    return -np.log(np.clip(b, 1e-9, 1 - 1e-12)) / dt

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
dt = 1 / 252
theta_hat = fitted_theta(simulate_ou(0.0, 1.0, 253, dt, 20_000, rng), dt)
half_life_days = np.log(2) / theta_hat * 252
print(f"random walk: median half-life {np.median(half_life_days):.0f} days, "
      f"{(half_life_days < 126).mean():.0%} under six months")
```

## Takeaway

The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process is the simplest honest model of something that gets pulled back, and its
parameters have physical meaning: a spring constant and a temperature. The hard part is not the model but the
speed of the spring. Estimate it on a short window and you will find a spring even in a random walk.
