A physics student meets the heat equation in their second year: heat spreads along a rod, sharp temperature
differences smooth out, and the solution is a Gaussian that widens with time. Option pricing looks like a
different world, with its own jargon. It is the same equation.

## The pricing equation

Under the Black–Scholes assumptions [1, 2] (the price follows geometric Brownian motion, trading is continuous, a
risk-free rate $r$ exists), the value $V(S, t)$ of a European option satisfies

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \tfrac12 \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}
+ r S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - r V = 0,
$$

with the payoff as a condition at expiry $T$: $V(S, T) = \max(S - K, 0)$ for a call. Note that the drift $\mu$
from the GBM note does not appear. Hedging removes it, which is the central surprise of the theory.

## Two changes of variable

Run time backwards from expiry, $\tau = T - t$, discount the value, and shift the log-price by the drift a hedged
position earns:

$$
V(S, t) = e^{-r\tau}\, u(x, \tau), \qquad x = \ln S + \left(r - \tfrac12 \sigma^2\right)\tau .
$$

Substituting and collecting terms, every first-derivative term cancels and what is left is

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial \tau} = \tfrac12 \sigma^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2},
\qquad u(x, 0) = \max(e^x - K, 0).
$$

That is the heat equation with diffusivity $\sigma^2/2$. The payoff is the initial temperature profile along the
rod, and the kink at the strike is a sharp edge that time smooths out.

<Callout label="Bachelier got there first">
Louis Bachelier's 1900 thesis [3] modeled prices with what we now call Brownian motion, five years before
Einstein's paper on Brownian motion [4]. Mathematicians drew on it, but economists overlooked it for about half a
century.
</Callout>

## Three ways to one number

The heat equation's solution is the initial profile averaged against a Gaussian kernel of variance $\sigma^2\tau$.
In financial language, the option price is the discounted average payoff over lognormal outcomes. Pick one
contract (spot 100, strike 110, rate 3%, volatility 60%, six months) and compute it three ways.

**1. Closed form.** For a call the Gaussian integral can be done exactly:

$$
C = S\,\Phi(d_1) - K e^{-rT}\Phi(d_2), \qquad
d_{1,2} = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r \pm \tfrac12\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} .
$$

It gives $C = 13.63$.

**2. Monte Carlo.** Draw the terminal price directly, average the discounted payoff. One million paths give
$13.61 \pm 0.03$. The error shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n}$ no matter how many dimensions the problem has, which is why
Monte Carlo wins for baskets and path-dependent payoffs and loses for a single vanilla option.

**3. Finite differences.** Put the heat equation on a grid in $x$ and step forward in $\tau$ with the simplest
explicit scheme:

$$
u_i^{n+1} = u_i^n + \lambda \left(u_{i+1}^n - 2u_i^n + u_{i-1}^n\right),
\qquad \lambda = \frac{\sigma^2 \Delta\tau}{2\,\Delta x^2}.
$$

With 400 grid points and $\lambda = 0.45$ it returns 13.63. Push $\lambda$ to 0.52 and the same code returns a
number around $-3 \times 10^{40}$.

<Callout label="The stability limit">
The explicit scheme is stable only for $\lambda \le \tfrac12$, the same limit a physics student meets when
simulating a cooling rod. Halving $\Delta x$ forces $\Delta\tau$ to quarter. Implicit schemes (Crank–Nicolson [5])
remove the limit at the cost of solving a tridiagonal system each step.
</Callout>

```python title="heat_pricer.py"
import numpy as np

def call_by_heat_equation(S, K, r, sigma, T, nx=400, lam=0.45):
    width = 6 * sigma * np.sqrt(T)
    x = np.linspace(np.log(S) - width, np.log(S) + width, nx)
    dx = x[1] - x[0]
    steps = int(np.ceil(T / (2 * lam * dx**2 / sigma**2)))
    dtau = T / steps
    lam = 0.5 * sigma**2 * dtau / dx**2           # actual λ after rounding the step count
    u = np.maximum(np.exp(x) - K, 0.0)            # payoff = initial temperature
    for _ in range(steps):
        u[1:-1] += lam * (u[2:] - 2 * u[1:-1] + u[:-2])
        u[0], u[-1] = 0.0, np.exp(x[-1]) - K       # far edges: worthless / deep in the money
    x0 = np.log(S) + (r - 0.5 * sigma**2) * T      # undo the change of variables
    return np.exp(-r * T) * np.interp(x0, x, u)

print(call_by_heat_equation(100, 110, 0.03, 0.60, 0.5))
```

## Which method when

| Method | Strength | Breaks when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Closed form | exact, instant | the payoff or the dynamics get realistic |
| Monte Carlo | any payoff, many assets | you need early exercise or smooth sensitivities |
| PDE grid | early exercise, barriers, fast in one or two dimensions | dimension grows; explicit steps break the stability limit |

## Takeaway

The equation that prices an option is the equation that cools a rod. Recognizing it buys you a century of
numerical methods, and the same lessons: Gaussians smooth corners, Monte Carlo converges slowly but anywhere,
and an explicit grid is fast until you take one step too many.
