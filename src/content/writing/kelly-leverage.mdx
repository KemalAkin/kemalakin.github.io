The [previous note](/writing/geometric-brownian-motion/) ended on an uncomfortable fact: a price that drifts up
6% a year with 38% volatility has a *median* that shrinks, because volatility drag ($\sigma^2/2 = 7.2\%$) is
larger than the drift. The obvious next question is practical. You don't have to put all your money in that
asset. If you can choose how much to hold, and even borrow to hold more, which amount makes the typical outcome
grow fastest?

The answer has a name, the Kelly criterion, and a one-line derivation.

## A coin first

Take a bet that pays even money and wins with probability $p = 0.55$. You stake a fraction $f$ of your wealth
each round. After $n$ rounds with $k$ wins, wealth is $W_n = W_0 (1+f)^k (1-f)^{n-k}$, so the growth rate per
round is

$$
G(f) = p \ln(1+f) + (1-p)\ln(1-f).
$$

Setting $G'(f) = 0$ gives $f^* = 2p - 1 = 10\%$, and a growth rate of about $0.50\%$ per round. Stake $20\%$
instead, twice as much, and the growth rate falls to almost exactly zero: twice the risk, none of the reward.
The rule comes from a 1956 paper by John Kelly, then at Bell Labs, on the information rate of noisy communication
channels [1]; the investing reading came later.

## The continuous version

Now hold a fraction $L$ of wealth in an asset that follows geometric Brownian motion, keep the rest in cash
earning $r$, and rebalance continuously. $L > 1$ means borrowing. Wealth itself follows a GBM, with drift
$r + L(\mu - r)$ and volatility $L\sigma$, so the result from the previous note applies directly: the median
grows at

$$
g(L) = r + L(\mu - r) - \tfrac{1}{2} L^2 \sigma^2 .
$$

The reward is linear in $L$ and the drag is quadratic. The parabola peaks at

$$
L^* = \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2}, \qquad g^* = r + \frac{(\mu - r)^2}{2\sigma^2}.
$$

Two consequences fall out of the parabola. Holding a fraction $c$ of the Kelly amount earns
$(2c - c^2)$ of the maximum excess growth, so **half Kelly keeps three quarters of the growth with half the
volatility**. And at $c = 2$ the excess growth is zero: you carry all the volatility for nothing.

## What it says about the hero's asset

With $\mu = 6\%$, $\sigma = 38\%$ and $r = 0$, Kelly says $L^* = 0.42$: keep 58% of the money in cash. The best
achievable median growth is a modest $1.25\%$ a year, while holding the asset outright ($L = 1$) shrinks the
median by $1.2\%$ a year.

A daily-rebalanced simulation (one year, 20,000 paths) shows what each choice does to the typical outcome:

| Leverage $L$ | Median wealth | Mean wealth | Paths below start |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.42 (Kelly) | 1.015 | 1.028 | 46% |
| 1 | 0.993 | 1.068 | 51% |
| 2 | 0.854 | 1.143 | 58% |
| 3 | 0.635 | 1.229 | 66% |

The mean keeps rising with leverage while the median collapses. A three-times leveraged, daily-rebalanced product
on this asset loses about a third of its value on a typical year even though its expected value goes *up*. This
is the "leveraged ETF decay" people complain about, and it needs no fees or tracking error to appear.

For a calmer asset the answer flips. A broad equity index with $\mu = 8\%$, $r = 4\%$ and $\sigma = 16\%$ gives
$L^* = 1.56$: Kelly says borrow. The criterion is not "use less leverage", it is "size by $\mu/\sigma^2$".

## Why nobody bets full Kelly

**The drift is the number you know worst.** The standard error of an estimated annual drift is about
$\sigma/\sqrt{T}$ for $T$ years of data: with 38% volatility and ten years of history that is $\pm 12\%$ around a
$6\%$ estimate. $L^*$ is linear in $\mu$, so the "optimal" leverage is uncertain by a factor of two or more.

**Drawdowns are deep even at the optimum.** In this model log-wealth is a Brownian motion with drift, and the
chance that wealth *ever* falls to a fraction $x$ of its starting value is

$$
\mathbb{P}(\text{ever reach } x W_0) = x^{\,2/c - 1}
$$

for a fraction $c$ of Kelly (with $r = 0$). At full Kelly the chance of halving at some point is $1/2$; at half
Kelly it is $1/8$. Most people discover they cannot sit through the full-Kelly path.

**The left wall is a cliff.** In the continuous model the growth curve is a symmetric parabola, so overbetting
and underbetting by the same amount cost the same. Real returns jump. In the coin version $G(f) \to -\infty$ as
$f \to 1$: a single bad outcome at high leverage is not a slower path, it is the end of the path.

So practitioners treat $L^*$ as a ceiling and run a fraction of it, often a half.

## Checking it numerically

```python title="kelly.py"
import numpy as np

def growth(L, mu, sigma, r=0.0):
    return r + L * (mu - r) - 0.5 * L**2 * sigma**2

def leveraged_paths(L, mu, sigma, days=252, n=20_000, seed=1):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    dt = 1 / 252
    z = rng.standard_normal((n, days))
    asset = np.exp((mu - 0.5 * sigma**2) * dt + sigma * np.sqrt(dt) * z) - 1
    return np.prod(1 + L * asset, axis=1)  # rebalanced to L every day

mu, sigma = 0.06, 0.38
L_star = mu / sigma**2
print(f"Kelly leverage {L_star:.2f}, max growth {growth(L_star, mu, sigma):.2%}")
for L in (L_star, 1, 2, 3):
    w = leveraged_paths(L, mu, sigma)
    print(f"L={L:.2f}  median {np.median(w):.3f}  mean {w.mean():.3f}  below start {(w < 1).mean():.0%}")
```

## Takeaway

The median path is the one you will most likely live through, and it grows at $\mu - \sigma^2/2$, not at
$\mu$. Leverage scales the first term and squares the second, which is why the same rule that says "borrow" for
an index fund says "keep most of it in cash" for a volatile coin.
