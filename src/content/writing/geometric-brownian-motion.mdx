The Monte Carlo panel on the home page draws 64 price paths a year long and stacks where they end into a
histogram. Watch it for a few runs and something looks off: the model is set to grow 6% a year, yet about half
of the paths end below where they started. Nothing is broken. That is what compounding does to a noisy price.

## The model

Geometric Brownian motion says the *relative* change of a price $S$ over a short time $dt$ is a constant drift
plus noise:

$$
dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW
$$

Here $\mu$ is the drift, $\sigma$ the volatility and $dW$ the increment of a Brownian motion, a normal draw with
variance $dt$. Applying Itô's lemma to $\ln S$ gives a closed-form solution,

$$
S_T = S_0 \exp\!\Big( \big(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\big)\,T + \sigma W_T \Big),
$$

so $\ln S_T$ is normal and $S_T$ is lognormal. The extra $-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2$ is the whole story.

## Mean and median part ways

Take the expectation and the noise term contributes $e^{\sigma^2 T/2}$, which exactly cancels the correction:

$$
\mathbb{E}[S_T] = S_0\, e^{\mu T}, \qquad \operatorname{median}(S_T) = S_0\, e^{(\mu - \sigma^2/2)\,T}.
$$

The mean grows at $\mu$; the typical path grows at $\mu - \sigma^2/2$. The gap, $\sigma^2/2$ per year, is
often called *volatility drag*. A few lucky paths that end far above the rest pull the mean up, while most paths
sit below it. The chance of ending below the starting price is

$$
\mathbb{P}(S_T < S_0) = \Phi\!\left( -\frac{(\mu - \sigma^2/2)\sqrt{T}}{\sigma} \right),
$$

with $\Phi$ the standard normal distribution function.

## Simulating it

Because the solution is exact, there is no need for a small-step integrator: draw normal increments, add the
drift in log space and exponentiate.

```python title="gbm.py"
import numpy as np

def gbm_paths(s0, mu, sigma, years=1.0, steps=252, n=400, seed=7):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    dt = years / steps
    shocks = rng.standard_normal((n, steps))
    log_steps = (mu - 0.5 * sigma**2) * dt + sigma * np.sqrt(dt) * shocks
    log_paths = np.cumsum(log_steps, axis=1)
    return s0 * np.exp(np.hstack([np.zeros((n, 1)), log_paths]))

paths = gbm_paths(100, mu=0.06, sigma=0.38, n=100_000)
final = paths[:, -1]
print(f"mean {final.mean():.1f}  median {np.median(final):.1f}  "
      f"below start {(final < 100).mean():.1%}")
```

With a large `n` the printout lands on the theory: mean near $106.2$, median near $98.8$, a little over half the
paths below the start.

## Why it matters

Volatility drag is why a portfolio's average return and its compounded return differ, why leveraged products
decay in sideways markets, and why "expected value" can describe almost nobody's actual outcome. The model is
deliberately simple; real prices have fat tails and changing volatility. The gap between mean and median survives
those refinements, and it only gets wider as volatility rises.
