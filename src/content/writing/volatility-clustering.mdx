The GBM and Kelly notes assume one fixed volatility $\sigma$. Look at any price chart and the assumption fails at
a glance: quiet weeks follow quiet weeks, and a violent day is usually followed by more violent days. Mandelbrot
noticed it in cotton prices in 1963: large changes tend to be followed by large changes, of either sign [1]. It is
called volatility clustering, and it is one of a handful of "stylized facts" that hold across almost every liquid
market [2]:

- daily returns are close to uncorrelated: yesterday's sign says little about today's;
- the *size* of returns ($|r|$ or $r^2$) is strongly and persistently correlated;
- the distribution of returns has fatter tails than a normal one.

The first fact is why prices look like random walks. The second is why risk is forecastable even when direction
is not.

## GARCH(1,1)

Engle's ARCH model [3] and Bollerslev's generalization, GARCH [4], make tomorrow's variance a function of today's
news. Write the daily return as $r_t = \sigma_t z_t$ with $z_t$ an independent standard normal draw, and let

$$
\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\, r_{t-1}^2 + \beta\, \sigma_{t-1}^2 .
$$

A large move yesterday ($\alpha$ term) raises today's variance, and the variance itself is sticky ($\beta$ term).
With $\alpha + \beta < 1$ the process settles around a long-run variance, and every shock fades geometrically:

$$
\bar\sigma^2 = \frac{\omega}{1 - \alpha - \beta}, \qquad
\text{half-life of a shock} = \frac{\ln \tfrac12}{\ln(\alpha + \beta)} \text{ days}.
$$

The sum $\alpha + \beta$ is the **persistence**. For daily data on most assets it lands between 0.9 and 0.99.

## Fat tails for free

Every $z_t$ is normal, yet the returns are not. Mixing normals with different variances fattens the tails, and
GARCH mixes them in a specific way. For normal shocks the kurtosis is

$$
\kappa = \frac{3\left(1 - (\alpha+\beta)^2\right)}{1 - (\alpha+\beta)^2 - 2\alpha^2} > 3,
$$

and the autocorrelation of squared returns starts at

$$
\rho_1 = \frac{\alpha\,(1 - \alpha\beta - \beta^2)}{1 - 2\alpha\beta - \beta^2}
$$

and then decays as $\rho_k = \rho_1 (\alpha+\beta)^{k-1}$.

Take $\alpha = 0.10$, $\beta = 0.85$ and a long-run volatility of 60% a year (a 365-day year, since crypto
trades every day). Then:

| Quantity | Theory | Simulation (200,000 days) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shock half-life | 13.5 days | |
| Kurtosis | 3.77 | 3.75 |
| Autocorrelation of $r$, lag 1 | 0 | −0.0005 |
| Autocorrelation of $r^2$, lags 1 / 5 / 20 | 0.179 / 0.146 / 0.068 | 0.180 / 0.144 / 0.067 |

Two parameters reproduce all three stylized facts. They do not reproduce the *amount* of tail seen in real
data, which is why practical fits usually replace the normal $z_t$ with a Student-$t$.

## The same facts in Bitcoin

The "BTC daily" tab uses Binance's BTCUSDT perpetual, daily closes from September 2021 to October 2026: 1,878
returns with an annualized volatility of 52%. All three facts are there:

- autocorrelation of returns at lags 1, 5 and 20 is −0.02, 0.02 and −0.04, inside the ±0.05 band that pure noise
  would produce with this many points;
- autocorrelation of squared returns is 0.18 at lag 1, 0.07 at lag 5 and zero by lag 20;
- kurtosis is 7.3, well above the normal 3.

The maximum-likelihood fit with normal shocks gives $\hat\alpha = 0.11$, $\hat\beta = 0.82$: persistence 0.93, a
shock half-life of about 10 days, and a long-run volatility of 54%. It reproduces the lag-1 correlation of squared
returns almost exactly (model 0.18) but expects more memory further out (0.14 at lag 5 against 0.07 in the data),
and its kurtosis is 3.7 against 7.3. After dividing each return by its fitted $\sigma_t$, the leftovers still have
kurtosis 6.2: the clustering is explained, the tails are not.

Swapping the normal shock for a Student-$t$ fixes the tails with a striking estimate: about 3 degrees of freedom,
barely enough for the variance to exist. The persistence then rises to 0.997, so in that fit shocks fade over
months rather than days and the long-run level is poorly pinned down.

## Fitting it

The model gives the likelihood directly: given the parameters, run the variance recursion over the data and
add up the normal log-densities. Maximizing that is a three-parameter optimization.

```python title="garch.py"
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

def simulate_garch(omega, alpha, beta, n, seed=11):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    r, var = np.empty(n), np.empty(n)
    var[0] = omega / (1 - alpha - beta)
    for t in range(n):
        if t > 0:
            var[t] = omega + alpha * r[t - 1] ** 2 + beta * var[t - 1]
        r[t] = np.sqrt(var[t]) * rng.standard_normal()
    return r

def neg_log_likelihood(params, r):
    omega, alpha, beta = params
    if omega <= 0 or alpha < 0 or beta < 0 or alpha + beta >= 1:
        return np.inf
    var = np.empty_like(r)
    var[0] = r.var()
    for t in range(1, len(r)):
        var[t] = omega + alpha * r[t - 1] ** 2 + beta * var[t - 1]
    return 0.5 * np.sum(np.log(var) + r**2 / var)

long_run_daily_var = 0.60**2 / 365
omega = long_run_daily_var * (1 - 0.10 - 0.85)
r = simulate_garch(omega, 0.10, 0.85, n=2_000)
fit = minimize(neg_log_likelihood, x0=[r.var() * 0.1, 0.05, 0.90], args=(r,), method="Nelder-Mead")
print(fit.x)
```

On 2,000 simulated days the fit returns $\hat\alpha = 0.12$, $\hat\beta = 0.83$ and a long-run volatility of
61%, close to the truth but not exact; individual parameters are noisier than their sum. For real work, the
`arch` package [5] does this with standard errors and other shock distributions.

## Volatility as a forecast

The payoff is a forecast of variance at every horizon. Iterating the recursion,

$$
\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\sigma_{t+h}^2\right] = \bar\sigma^2 + (\alpha+\beta)^{h-1}\left(\sigma_{t+1}^2 - \bar\sigma^2\right),
$$

so after a shock, near-term volatility is high and the forecast slides back to the long-run level with the
half-life above. That shape, a term structure of volatility, is what risk limits, margin and option prices are
really about.

It also closes a loop with the Kelly note. The growth-optimal leverage is $\mu/\sigma^2$; if $\sigma$ moves, so
should position size. Scaling exposure by forecast volatility, "volatility targeting", is Kelly with a
time-varying $\sigma$ and an honest admission that $\mu$ is unknown.

## Takeaway

Direction is close to noise; magnitude is not. A model with two numbers, how hard news hits and how long it
lingers, explains why calm and storm come in runs, why tails are fat even with normal shocks, and gives a risk
forecast you can size positions with.
