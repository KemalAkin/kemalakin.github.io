Every portfolio optimizer starts with a covariance matrix estimated from history. With $N$ assets that matrix has
$N(N+1)/2$ numbers in it: 5,050 for a hundred assets. A year of daily returns gives $T = 250$ observations per
asset. Something has to give, and random matrix theory says exactly what.

## A physicist's null hypothesis

In the 1950s Wigner faced a similar problem with heavy atomic nuclei: too many interacting parts to model, but a
spectrum of energy levels that could be measured [1, 2]. His move was to ask what the spectrum of a *random* matrix
looks like, and compare. Deviations from the random baseline are where the physics is.

For correlation matrices the baseline is the Marchenko–Pastur law [3], and physicists first compared it with
stock-market correlations in 1999 [4, 5]. If the $N$ series were pure,
independent noise, the eigenvalues of their sample correlation matrix would not all be 1. They would spread over
a band that depends only on the ratio $q = N/T$:

$$
\lambda_\pm = \left(1 \pm \sqrt{q}\right)^2, \qquad
\rho(\lambda) = \frac{\sqrt{(\lambda_+ - \lambda)(\lambda - \lambda_-)}}{2\pi q \lambda}
\quad \text{for } \lambda_- \le \lambda \le \lambda_+ .
$$

For $N = 100$ and $T = 250$, $q = 0.4$ and the noise band runs from **0.14 to 2.66**. A sample of pure noise
lands where the law says: its eigenvalues ran from 0.15 to 2.54 in one draw. Any eigenvalue inside the band is
indistinguishable from noise.

## What survives

Real returns are not independent: there is a market, there are sectors. In a simulated market with one market
factor and four sectors, the true correlation matrix has one large eigenvalue (about 26, the market) and four
sector eigenvalues around 2.0–2.3. The sector eigenvalues sit *inside* the noise band. With one year of data
they are real but invisible; on average only about two eigenvalues of the sample matrix rise above $\lambda_+$.

## A hundred crypto assets

The "100 crypto perps" tab uses real data: daily log returns of the 100 largest Binance crypto perpetuals by
traded value, over the 250 days to October 2026. That is $N = 100$, $T = 250$, the same $q = 0.4$ as the
simulation.

The top eigenvalue is **30.5**: a single "crypto market" direction carries 30% of the total variance. Against the
pure-noise edge of 2.66, it is the only eigenvalue that clearly stands out.

There is a subtlety. Once the market mode takes its 30%, the bulk only carries the remaining 70% of the variance,
and the noise band shrinks with it: the edge moves from 2.66 to about 1.85. Against that edge, five more eigenvalues (2.44, 2.22, 2.13, 2.02 and 1.87)
poke out. They are candidates for real structure such as groups of coins that move together, but they sit close
to the edge, and with one year of data that is weak evidence. Everything else is indistinguishable from noise.

## Why an optimizer cares

The minimum-variance portfolio is $w \propto \Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}$. Inverting a matrix amplifies its smallest
eigenvalues, and the smallest sample eigenvalues are exactly the ones noise pushed too low. The optimizer
reads them as "almost riskless combinations" and loads up on them.

For pure noise the damage has a closed form [6, 7]. The portfolio's in-sample variance is $(1 - q)$ times the true
minimum, and its true (out-of-sample) variance is $1/(1-q)$ times it. With $q = 0.4$ the simulation gives 0.60
and 1.66 against a theory of 0.60 and 1.67. **The variance you report is nearly three times smaller than the
variance you take.**

## Cleaning by clipping

The simplest repair, eigenvalue clipping [8], keeps the eigenvalues above $\lambda_+$ and replaces everything in
the bulk with their average, which preserves the trace. The eigenvectors are untouched.

In the factor-model simulation:

| Covariance used | True variance of the min-variance portfolio |
| --- | --- |
| Sample | 1.67 × optimal |
| Clipped | 1.17 × optimal |

Clipping removes most of the excess risk with a few lines of code and no tuning parameter. Better estimators
exist, such as Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage [9] and the rotationally invariant estimators reviewed by Bun, Bouchaud and
Potters [7], but they share the idea: don't trust the bulk.

```python title="clip.py"
import numpy as np

def clip_correlation(returns):
    """Keep eigenvalues above the Marchenko–Pastur edge, flatten the rest."""
    T, N = returns.shape
    corr = np.corrcoef(returns, rowvar=False)
    edge = (1 + np.sqrt(N / T)) ** 2
    eigval, eigvec = np.linalg.eigh(corr)
    noise = eigval < edge
    eigval[noise] = eigval[noise].mean()          # trace preserved
    cleaned = (eigvec * eigval) @ eigvec.T
    d = np.sqrt(np.diag(cleaned))
    return cleaned / np.outer(d, d), int((~noise).sum())

def min_variance_weights(cov):
    w = np.linalg.solve(cov, np.ones(len(cov)))
    return w / w.sum()
```

To use it in an optimizer, rebuild a covariance from the cleaned correlation and the sample volatilities.

## Takeaway

A sample correlation matrix is a few real directions floating on a sea of noise, and random matrix theory draws
the waterline. Below it, the right move is to stop pretending you know anything, and an optimizer that is
forced to admit that takes noticeably less hidden risk.
