Equity market data is a product: you buy a feed from the exchange or a vendor, and the license decides what you
may do with it. Crypto exchanges give theirs away. Every large venue publishes its trades, candles and order book
to anyone with an internet connection, no account needed. So the question is not where to buy data but how to take
it. The exchanges offer four doors, each with a different contract about who holds the state, who pays for
gaps, and how far behind the market you are. Around them sit two shortcuts: a library that speaks to every
exchange in one dialect, and vendors who have already done the collecting.

This note walks through all six with Binance [1, 2] and OKX [3] as examples. Every limit quoted is from the
providers' documentation as of November 2026; they change often enough that the date matters.

## 1. REST: ask when you need it

A REST endpoint answers one question per request: the last 1,000 trades, the order book right now, the candles
between two timestamps. The connection carries no memory between requests, which is what makes REST easy to reason
about. A failed request can simply be repeated, a job can be rerun from any point, and the code is a loop around
an HTTP call.

The price is the rate limit. Binance counts a **weight** per request against a budget per IP address: 6,000 per
minute on the spot API, where a page of candles costs 2 and a full 5,000-level order book costs 250 [1]. Exceed it
and you get HTTP 429 [5]; keep going after the 429s and the IP is banned with status 418, for anything from two
minutes to three days. OKX sets a limit per endpoint instead: 40 requests per 2 seconds for current candles, 20
for the history endpoint [3].

For history, REST is fine. A week of one-minute BTC/USDT candles is 10,080 rows: 11 requests to Binance at up to
1,000 rows each, 34 to OKX at up to 300.

```python title="candles.py"
import time
import requests

def binance_klines(symbol, interval, start_ms, end_ms):
    """Page forward through /api/v3/klines, 1,000 candles per request (weight 2 each)."""
    out, t = [], start_ms
    while t < end_ms:
        rows = requests.get("https://data-api.binance.vision/api/v3/klines",
                            params={"symbol": symbol, "interval": interval, "startTime": t,
                                    "endTime": end_ms - 1, "limit": 1000}, timeout=10).json()
        if not rows:
            break
        out += rows
        t = rows[-1][0] + 1                  # the next page starts after the last open time
    return out

def okx_candles(inst_id, bar, start_ms, end_ms):
    """Page backwards through /api/v5/market/history-candles, 300 per request, newest first."""
    out, after = [], end_ms
    while True:
        rows = requests.get("https://openapi.okx.com/api/v5/market/history-candles",
                            params={"instId": inst_id, "bar": bar, "after": after, "limit": 300},
                            timeout=10).json()["data"]
        rows = [r for r in rows if int(r[0]) >= start_ms]
        if not rows:
            break
        out += rows
        after = rows[-1][0]                   # "after" means older than this timestamp
        time.sleep(0.1)                       # stay under 20 requests per 2 seconds
    return out[::-1]

end = 1_793_491_200_000                       # 2026-11-01 00:00 UTC
b = binance_klines("BTCUSDT", "1m", end - 7 * 86_400_000, end)
o = okx_candles("BTC-USDT", "1m", end - 7 * 86_400_000, end)
```

Binance asks public-data clients to use the `data-api.binance.vision` host [1]; the code does. The two loops
already show the small differences that cause bugs: the symbol is `BTCUSDT` on one venue and
`BTC-USDT` on the other, one paginates forward and the other backwards, and one end bound is inclusive. The
data agrees well: over that week the two venues' one-minute closes differed by a median of 0.7 basis points and at
most 9.6.

## 2. WebSocket: let the exchange tell you

REST answers when you ask. For anything happening now, asking is the wrong model. A poller sees the market only
at the moments it polls, and if more happened between two polls than one response holds, some of it is gone.

<PollingExplorer />

The figure replays 30 minutes of Binance BTC/USDT spot trades, recorded from the public stream on a quiet Saturday
evening: 8,155 trades, an average of 4.5 a second, but 436 seconds with none and one second with 466. Trading
comes in bursts, and the bursts are what break a poller.

- **Delay.** Polling once a second, the median trade reaches you 0.6 s after it happened: half the interval plus
  the response's trip back. The stream delivered the same trades a median 71 ms after their exchange timestamp.
- **Gaps.** The recent-trades endpoint returns at most 1,000 trades, 500 by default. Ask for 100 and poll every
  second, and 20% of the trades were never seen, all of them inside bursts. Poll every 30 s with the default 500 and
  you lose 4.7%.
- **Budget.** Each poll costs 25 weight. Four polls a second use the whole 6,000-per-minute budget of the IP, and
  still deliver trades a median 0.27 s late, about four times slower than the stream.

A poller can be made complete by paging through older trades by ID, but it cannot be made fast: its delay is set
by its interval, and the interval by the rate limit.

<Callout label="How the delays were measured">
Stream delay is the local receive time minus the exchange's trade timestamp, on a laptop clock kept by NTP, so
it carries the clock's error (tens of milliseconds). Polling delay is computed by replaying the recorded trades
against a poll schedule, plus half of the 285 ms median round trip I measured to the trades endpoint over a
kept-alive connection. Opening a new HTTPS connection for each request took 0.4 s to 1.1 s, depending on the
host: reuse the connection.
</Callout>

A WebSocket [4] turns the direction around: one long-lived connection over which the exchange pushes every event
as it happens. One connection can carry many streams (up to 1,024 on Binance [2]), and nothing is spent on
requests you did not need.

What you give up is statelessness. The connection now has a lifecycle you must manage:

| | Binance [2] | OKX [3] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Keep-alive | server pings every 20 s, pong within 1 min | send `ping` before 30 s of silence |
| Disconnected | at 24 hours, always | after 30 s with no data |
| Client limits | 5 messages / s per connection | 3 connections / s; 480 (un)subscribe or login / h per connection |
| Order book | REST snapshot, then diffs every 100 ms | 400-level snapshot, then diffs every 100 ms |

The order book is where this matters most. For more than the top few levels, neither exchange resends the book on
every change; they send a snapshot once and then only the levels that changed. Your copy of the book is correct only if you applied every
diff, in order, to the right snapshot, so both venues number their messages and expect you to check.

Binance's documented procedure [2]: open the diff stream and buffer it, fetch a REST snapshot, drop buffered events
the snapshot already contains, then apply each event only if its first update ID `U` is the previous event's last
ID `u` plus one. Anything else means you missed a message, and the book must be thrown away and rebuilt. OKX
marks each message with `seqId` and `prevSeqId`; a message whose `prevSeqId` is not the last `seqId` you saw is a
gap. OKX's messages also carry a `checksum` field, but the documentation now says it is fixed to 0 and must not be
used: sequence numbers replaced it. Code written against the older documentation still compiles and silently
checks nothing.

```python title="local_book.py"
def apply(book, event):
    """One step of Binance's local order book procedure. Returns False on a gap: rebuild from a new snapshot."""
    if event["u"] <= book["id"]:
        return True                           # already in the snapshot
    if event["U"] > book["id"] + 1:
        return False                          # missed at least one message
    for side, key in (("bids", "b"), ("asks", "a")):
        for price, qty in event[key]:
            if float(qty) == 0:
                book[side].pop(price, None)   # zero quantity removes the level
            else:
                book[side][price] = qty
    book["id"] = event["u"]
    return True
```

Run for the same 30 minutes, both checks passed. On Binance, 17,878 diff events were applied on top of one
snapshot, a dozen older ones were discarded as the procedure says, and no gap appeared. On OKX, each of 12,580
messages had a `prevSeqId` matching the `seqId` before it. A quiet half hour on a home
connection proves little about a volatile day, which is exactly when a gap costs most; the check has to stay in.

Trades need the same care in a different form. Binance's raw trade stream numbers every trade, and in the recording
the IDs ran without a break. OKX's trades channel sends one message per taker order and price, aggregating the
fills inside it [3], so its trade IDs skip by design: in 770 messages the ID jumped 93 times. A gap check written
for one venue raises false alarms on the other.

## 3. FIX and binary feeds: the institutional door

Traditional electronic trading has its own protocol, FIX, maintained by the FIX Trading Community, the industry's
nonprofit standards body. Binance offers it on the spot market [1]: FIX 4.4 messages over a TLS connection, in three
kinds of session. Order entry places and cancels orders; drop copy receives a copy of the account's executions,
delayed by one second; market data streams quotes and trades. Two details mark it as the institutional door: FIX
sessions accept only Ed25519 API keys, and the same sessions can answer in Simple Binary Encoding (SBE) [6] instead
of FIX text, on ports 9001 and 9002.

SBE is also available without FIX. Binance's SBE market-data streams send public market data as binary
messages with timestamps in microseconds, and need an Ed25519 API key even though the data is public [1]. OKX publishes SBE order-book and trade channels on a separate WebSocket endpoint, but its tick-by-tick
full-depth channels (`books-l2-tbt`, `books50-l2-tbt`) are open only to accounts in fee tier VIP4 and above [3].

The pattern is the same on both venues: the free JSON feed is good enough to learn from and to build most research
on, and the fastest, finest-grained feeds sit behind an API key or a trading volume.

## 4. Archives: the data someone already collected

For research, the fastest API is often no API. Binance publishes its public market data as daily and monthly ZIP
files at data.binance.vision: klines, trades and aggregated trades, for spot and for both kinds of futures [2].
Daily files appear the next day. Each file comes with a SHA-256 checksum, and the archive keeps a list of files it has
replaced after finding problems, which is worth reading before trusting any one file.

```python title="archive.py"
import hashlib, io, zipfile
import requests

base = "https://data.binance.vision/data/spot/daily/klines/BTCUSDT/1m/"
name = "BTCUSDT-1m-2026-10-31.zip"
blob = requests.get(base + name, timeout=30).content
expected = requests.get(base + name + ".CHECKSUM", timeout=30).text.split()[0]
assert hashlib.sha256(blob).hexdigest() == expected, "corrupt download"

with zipfile.ZipFile(io.BytesIO(blob)) as z:
    rows = z.read(z.namelist()[0]).decode().splitlines()
print(len(rows), rows[0].split(",")[0])     # 1440 1793404800000000
```

That last number is a trap. The REST API returns milliseconds; spot files from 1 January 2025 onwards are in
**microseconds** [2]. Mix the two and the dates come out wrong by a factor of a thousand. Nothing fails, so
the error can sit in a dataset for a long time.

## 5. Libraries: one client, many exchanges

Everything so far has been one venue at a time, and the venues disagree on symbols, pagination, units and error
codes. The open-source library ccxt [7] exists to hide that: one interface to more than a hundred exchanges, in
eight languages with Python among them, under the MIT license. Symbols become `BTC/USDT` everywhere,
timestamps are milliseconds everywhere, and a rate limiter, on by default, paces requests for you. A research
terminal I work on reads its exchange data through it.

The candle download from section 1 becomes one function for both venues:

```python title="ccxt_candles.py"
import ccxt

def fetch_range(exchange, symbol, start_ms, end_ms):
    out, since = [], start_ms
    while since < end_ms:
        rows = exchange.fetch_ohlcv(symbol, "1m", since=since, limit=1000)
        rows = [r for r in rows if r[0] < end_ms]
        if not rows:
            break
        out += rows
        since = rows[-1][0] + 60_000          # next minute after the last candle
    return out

end = 1_793_491_200_000                       # 2026-11-01 00:00 UTC
for exchange in (ccxt.binance(), ccxt.okx()):
    candles = fetch_range(exchange, "BTC/USDT", end - 7 * 86_400_000, end)
```

It returned the same 10,080 candles per venue as the hand-written loops, identical in every open, high, low,
close and volume. On OKX, ccxt translated the forward paging into the backward `after`/`before` parameters and
capped each page at 300 by itself: 11 requests to Binance, 34 to OKX, exactly as before.

The abstraction holds for the common case and leaks at the edges. Three places I checked:

- **Automatic pagination.** ccxt can page for you with `params={"paginate": True}`, which its manual marks as
  experimental [7]. For the same week it returned 10,000 of the 10,080 candles on Binance, stopping at the default
  of 10 requests without an error, and on OKX it raised an error asking for a larger `paginationCalls`. One flag,
  two behaviors, and the quiet one is the dangerous one.
- **Order books.** The WebSocket half, ccxt Pro, is a free part of the library [8]. Its `watch_order_book`
  keeps the local book for you, and in the source of version 4.5.92 it implements both venues' sequence checks
  from section 2: `U` and `u` on Binance, `prevSeqId` on OKX. A gap surfaces as an exception, but a different one
  per venue (`ChecksumError` on Binance, `InvalidNonce` on OKX), so the reconnect logic still has to know about
  both. Over a 30-second test both books stayed in sync with no errors.
- **Venue-specific fields.** What only one exchange has does not fit a unified shape. Most unified structures
  keep the exchange's original reply in an `info` field, and code that reaches into it is venue-specific again.

A library buys breadth, not depth. When one venue matters more than the others, its own documentation is still
the place to read what a field means.

## 6. Aggregators: someone else runs the collectors

The last option is not to talk to exchanges at all. Two quite different kinds of business sell (or give away)
data that someone else has collected.

**Price aggregators** publish one price per coin, combined from many venues. CoinGecko's API [9] works without a
key or with a free demo key at 100 calls a minute, with a granularity that depends on the range asked for:
five-minutely for the last day, hourly up to 90 days, daily beyond. The demo plan reaches back 365 days. I
compared its last 24 hours of bitcoin prices (to 8 November 00:24 UTC, 288 points) with Binance's BTC/USDT
one-minute closes. CoinGecko was a median 9.3 basis points lower. The difference is not an error but a currency:
CoinGecko quotes US dollars, Binance quotes tether, and over that day CoinGecko priced one tether at 0.9992
dollars. Converting at that rate brings the median difference to −1.1 basis points, with 80% of points within 3.
The quote currency is part of the price, and a stablecoin is not quite the dollar.

An aggregated price is the right input for valuing a portfolio of a hundred coins. It is the wrong input for
anything about execution: it describes no order book you could trade against.

**Tick-data vendors** record the exchanges' own feeds and sell the history. Tardis.dev [10] records the WebSocket
feeds of more than 50 exchanges and sells them both as the original messages, to replay, and as normalized CSV
files. The first day of every month is free to download without a key, which makes it easy to check. For
1 November, its Binance BTC/USDT trades file and Binance's own archive held the same 1,147,208 trades, equal in
ID, microsecond timestamp, price, quantity and side. Its recorder stamps each message on arrival, a median 3 ms
after the exchange's timestamp: the recorder runs in Tokyo, the region the vendor's documentation gives for
Binance's own servers [10]. My home connection, for comparison, saw 71 ms.

For trades, then, a vendor sells what the exchange already gives away. What it adds is what the exchange does
not keep. Binance's spot archive has trades, aggregated trades and candles but no order book, and its REST API
serves only the book as it is now. Tardis.dev has the BTC/USDT book, every change since 2019, checked with the
same `U`/`u` sequence numbers as in section 2 while it was recorded [10]. A book that nobody recorded cannot be
fetched later. Institutional vendors such as Kaiko [11] go further, with reference rates and indices for
valuation; its indices are administered under the IOSCO principles for financial benchmarks.

## Choosing

| You want | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Years of candles or trades for a backtest | the archive, then REST for the days it does not have yet |
| A dashboard that refreshes every minute | REST, with the rate limit in mind |
| Every trade, or a live order book | WebSocket, with gap detection and reconnects |
| The lowest latency, or order entry at scale | FIX or SBE, with the keys and tiers they require |
| One codebase across many exchanges | ccxt, knowing where it leaks |
| Prices for many coins, to value a portfolio | a price aggregator, in the currency you report in |
| Order book history you did not record | a tick-data vendor |

Whatever you choose, reread the documentation now and then. While I was writing this note, OKX's documentation
said that the API domain depends on the region an account was registered in and that the order-book checksum is
now always zero; Binance's archive had switched to microseconds at the start of 2025. None of these changes makes
old code fail loudly.
