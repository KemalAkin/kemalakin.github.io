You hold a position you need to get rid of: a fund rebalancing, a desk unwinding inventory, a liquidation. Sell
it all at once and you eat through the order book, paying a worse price on every unit. Sell it slowly and the
price has time to move against you. Somewhere between "now" and "eventually" is a best schedule. Almgren and Chriss
[1] showed that finding it is the same kind of problem as finding the path of a particle.

## Two costs pulling in opposite directions

Let $x(t)$ be the inventory still to sell, from $x(0) = X$ to $x(T) = 0$, so the selling rate is $-\dot x$.

**Impact.** Trading fast moves the price you get. The simplest model charges a concession proportional to the
rate, $\eta\,|\dot x|$ per unit, so the expected cost of the schedule is

$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{cost}] = \eta \int_0^T \dot x^2 \, dt .
$$

**Risk.** Inventory you still hold is exposed to the price, which moves with volatility $\sigma$. The variance
of the proceeds is

$$
\operatorname{Var}[\text{cost}] = \sigma^2 \int_0^T x^2 \, dt .
$$

A trader who dislikes risk with aversion $\lambda$ minimizes $\mathbb{E} + \lambda \operatorname{Var}$:

$$
\min_{x(\cdot)} \int_0^T \left( \eta\,\dot x^2 + \lambda\sigma^2 x^2 \right) dt .
$$

## The Euler–Lagrange equation

That integral is an action. The Euler–Lagrange equation for it is

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\left(2\eta\,\dot x\right) = 2\lambda\sigma^2 x
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\ddot x = \kappa^2 x, \qquad \kappa^2 = \frac{\lambda\sigma^2}{\eta}.
$$

It is the equation of motion of a harmonic oscillator in imaginary time, or the temperature profile along a
cooling fin. With the boundary conditions $x(0) = X$, $x(T) = 0$ the solution is

$$
x(t) = X\,\frac{\sinh\big(\kappa (T - t)\big)}{\sinh(\kappa T)} .
$$

A single number controls the shape: $\kappa T$, the horizon measured in units of the trader's natural time scale
$1/\kappa$. As $\kappa T \to 0$ (no risk aversion, or a short horizon) the hyperbolic sine becomes a straight
line and the schedule is a constant-rate sale, TWAP. As $\kappa T$ grows, the schedule front-loads: sell hard
early, then taper off exponentially with time constant $1/\kappa$.

## What the urgency buys

Integrating the solution gives the cost and the risk relative to TWAP in closed form:

$$
\frac{\mathbb{E}}{\mathbb{E}_{\text{TWAP}}} = \frac{(\kappa T)^2}{\sinh^2 \kappa T}\left(\frac12 + \frac{\sinh 2\kappa T}{4\kappa T}\right),
$$

$$
\frac{\operatorname{Var}}{\operatorname{Var}_{\text{TWAP}}} = \frac{3}{\sinh^2 \kappa T}\left(\frac{\sinh 2\kappa T}{4\kappa T} - \frac12\right).
$$

| $\kappa T$ | Expected cost (× TWAP) | Risk, std. dev. (× TWAP) | Inventory left at $T/2$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 (TWAP) | 1.00 | 1.00 | 50% |
| 1 | 1.02 | 0.94 | 44% |
| 2 | 1.19 | 0.81 | 32% |
| 3 | 1.55 | 0.70 | 21% |
| 5 | 2.50 | 0.55 | 8% |

The first row of that table is the useful one. Going from TWAP to $\kappa T = 1$ cuts risk by 6% for 2% more
expected cost. Near TWAP the frontier is flat, for the same reason the Kelly parabola is flat at its top: at an
optimum, a small step away costs nothing to first order. The price of urgency rises steeply after that.

## Code

```python title="execution.py"
import numpy as np

def schedule(k_T, n=11):
    """Fraction of the position left at n evenly spaced times, for urgency k_T = kappa * T."""
    t = np.linspace(0, 1, n)
    if k_T < 1e-9:
        return 1 - t
    return np.sinh(k_T * (1 - t)) / np.sinh(k_T)

def cost_and_risk(k_T):
    """Expected impact cost and standard deviation, both relative to TWAP."""
    s2, q = np.sinh(k_T) ** 2, np.sinh(2 * k_T) / (4 * k_T)
    return k_T**2 / s2 * (0.5 + q), np.sqrt(3 / s2 * (q - 0.5))

for k_T in (1, 2, 3, 5):
    cost, risk = cost_and_risk(k_T)
    print(f"kT={k_T}: cost x{cost:.2f}, risk x{risk:.2f}, left at T/2 {schedule(k_T, 3)[1]:.0%}")
```

## What the model leaves out

Impact here is linear and instantaneous; in real markets it grows roughly like the square root of the traded size
[2] and decays over time after you trade. Volatility is constant, and the price has no drift, so the schedule never tries to time the market. Each
of these has its own literature, but the core result survives: the optimal schedule balances a cost that punishes
speed against a risk that punishes delay, and the balance point is a hyperbolic sine.
