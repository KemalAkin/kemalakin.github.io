The [heat-equation note](/writing/black-scholes-heat-equation/) priced an option with one number for volatility.
Ask the market for that number by inverting its option prices, and you get a different answer at every strike:
plotted against strike, implied volatility bends into a smile or slopes into a skew. A constant $\sigma$ cannot
produce that. The [GARCH note](/writing/volatility-clustering/) already showed volatility is not constant; the
Heston model [1] makes it random in continuous time and keeps option prices computable.

## The model

Price and variance each follow their own diffusion, and the two noises are correlated:

$$
\begin{aligned}
dS &= \mu S\,dt + \sqrt{v}\, S\, dW_1, \\[1ex]
dv &= \kappa(\theta - v)\,dt + \xi \sqrt{v}\, dW_2, \qquad d\langle W_1, W_2\rangle = \rho\, dt .
\end{aligned}
$$

The variance is pulled toward a long-run level $\theta$ at speed $\kappa$, like the
[Ornstein–Uhlenbeck](/writing/ornstein-uhlenbeck/) process, but its noise shrinks as $\sqrt v$, so it cannot go
far below zero. If $2\kappa\theta \ge \xi^2$, known as the Feller condition after Feller's study of this
diffusion [2], it never reaches zero at all. This is the square-root process of the Cox–Ingersoll–Ross
interest-rate model [3], borrowed for variance.

## What each parameter does to the smile

Three-month options, spot 100, current and long-run volatility both 60%, $\kappa = 2$, $\xi = 1$. Implied
volatility by strike:

| Strike | 70 | 80 | 90 | 100 | 110 | 120 | 130 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\rho = -0.7$ | 66.3 | 63.2 | 60.4 | 57.7 | 55.3 | 53.0 | 51.1 |
| $\rho = 0$ | 60.5 | 59.5 | 58.9 | 58.8 | 58.9 | 59.3 | 59.7 |
| $\rho = +0.3$ | 57.2 | 57.5 | 58.2 | 59.3 | 60.4 | 61.6 | 62.7 |
| $\rho = -0.7$, $\xi = 0.3$ | 62.2 | 61.2 | 60.3 | 59.6 | 58.8 | 58.2 | 57.6 |

**Correlation $\rho$ tilts it.** With $\rho < 0$, falls in price come with rises in volatility, which fattens the
left tail of the return distribution; low strikes become expensive and the curve slopes down. Flip the sign and
the skew flips.

**Vol of vol $\xi$ bends it.** It sets how much the variance moves and so how fat both tails are. Cut it from 1
to 0.3 and the same correlation gives a skew less than half as steep.

**The level sits below 60%.** Even with $\rho = 0$, at-the-money volatility is 58.8%, not 60%. Implied
volatility near the money tracks the average of $\sqrt v$, and by Jensen's inequality that is below
$\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[v]}$.

## Pricing without a closed form

Heston's contribution was computability. The characteristic function of $\ln S_T$ is known in closed form, and a
call price is two one-dimensional Fourier integrals of it:

$$
C = S\,P_1 - K e^{-rT} P_2, \qquad
P_j = \frac12 + \frac1\pi \int_0^\infty \operatorname{Re}\!\left[\frac{e^{-iu\ln K}\, f_j(u)}{iu}\right] du .
$$

The textbook form of $f_j$ has a branch-cut problem: the complex logarithm inside it can jump, and the integral
silently returns a wrong price for long maturities. The rearranged form used here and in the figure is proven
stable by Albrecher, Mayer, Schoutens and Tistaert [4].

<Callout label="A Monte Carlo cross-check, and a trap">
A simple Euler simulation of the variance, with negative values truncated to zero in both the drift and the noise
("full truncation" [5]), priced the 90-strike call at
$17.16 \pm 0.05$ with 250 time steps. The Fourier price is $17.08$: the simulation is biased, by almost two standard
errors. With 1,000 steps it gives $17.07 \pm 0.03$. The bias comes from discretizing a process that lives near
zero, and it is why Heston simulation has its own literature of better schemes [6].
</Callout>

```python title="heston.py"
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import quad

def char_fn(u, S, T, r, v0, kappa, theta, xi, rho):
    """Characteristic function of ln S_T, in the form that avoids the branch cut."""
    b = kappa - rho * xi * 1j * u
    d = np.sqrt(b**2 + xi**2 * (1j * u + u**2))
    g = (b - d) / (b + d)
    e = np.exp(-d * T)
    C = r * 1j * u * T + kappa * theta / xi**2 * ((b - d) * T - 2 * np.log((1 - g * e) / (1 - g)))
    D = (b - d) / xi**2 * (1 - e) / (1 - g * e)
    return np.exp(C + D * v0 + 1j * u * np.log(S))

def heston_call(S, K, T, r, *params):
    phi = lambda u: char_fn(u, S, T, r, *params)
    forward = phi(-1j)                           # E[S_T] = S e^{rT}
    p1 = quad(lambda u: (np.exp(-1j * u * np.log(K)) * phi(u - 1j) / (1j * u * forward)).real, 1e-8, 200, limit=400)[0]
    p2 = quad(lambda u: (np.exp(-1j * u * np.log(K)) * phi(u) / (1j * u)).real, 1e-8, 200, limit=400)[0]
    return S * (0.5 + p1 / np.pi) - K * np.exp(-r * T) * (0.5 + p2 / np.pi)

print(heston_call(100, 90, 0.25, 0.0, 0.36, 2.0, 0.36, 1.0, -0.7))   # about 17.08
```

## What Heston gets right and wrong

It produces skew and smile from two interpretable parameters and prices vanillas fast enough to calibrate. It
struggles with very short maturities, where real smiles are steeper than a diffusion for variance can make them;
that gap is one reason for adding jumps and for newer models of the variance. As a first model of why the smile
exists, it is the right one; Gatheral's book [7] is a practitioner's tour of it and its extensions.
